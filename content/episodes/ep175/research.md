# ep175 research: The 93-Billion-Light-Year Paradox (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.7-flash, gemini-3.8-flash, gemini-flash-latest) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: If the universe is only 13.8 billion years old and nothing travels faster than light, you might assume our cosmic bubble is at most 27.6 billion light-years across. In reality, the observable universe spans roughly 93 billion light-years in diameter. This mind-bending gap is not an error or a breach of Einstein's cosmic speed limit. When the oldest light we can see was emitted 380,000 years after the Big Bang, its source was only about 40 million light-years away. But as those photons spent 13.8 billion years journeying toward Earth, the fabric of space itself expanded relentlessly behind and around them. Because space can stretch faster than the speed of light without breaking any local laws of physics, those ancient sources have been carried 46.5 billion light-years away from us today, creating an observable sphere containing trillions of receding galaxies.

## Disputed or left out

- Total galaxy count: older surveys based on Hubble data estimate 100 to 200 billion galaxies, whereas later models estimate up to 2 trillion galaxies; a script should state estimates range from hundreds of billions up to 2 trillion.
- Exact diameter value: NASA sometimes rounds to 92 billion light-years in public educational explainers, while academic literature typically rounds to 93 billion light-years; a script should state 'between 92 and 93 billion light-years.'
- Global geometry: CMB data indicates space is flat, but some gravitational lensing studies hint at a closed, spherical geometry; a script should attribute flatness as the consensus model while noting curvature remains under study.
