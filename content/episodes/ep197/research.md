# ep197 research: The Planet Where One Day Lasts Two Years (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-19 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.7-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: For nearly a century, astronomers were certain that Mercury was tidally locked to the Sun, dragging one face through perpetual daylight and the other through frozen night. Because its orbital year was 88 Earth days, mapmakers assumed its rotation was identical. But in the 1960s, scientists bounced radar beams off Mercury and measured an unexpected spin rate of roughly 59 days. Italian mathematician Giuseppe Colombo cracked the mystery: Mercury wasn't locked 1:1, but in a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance, completing three axial rotations for every two orbits around the Sun. Because of this cosmic geometry, a single solar day from sunrise to sunrise on Mercury lasts 176 Earth days—precisely two full Mercury years. The myth had survived so long simply because whenever Mercury was best placed for telescopic observation from Earth, the orbital geometry happened to reveal the exact same side.

## Disputed or left out

- Volcanic plains vs. impact melt: Lunar geologists debated for decades whether Mercury's smooth plains were caused by volcanic lava flows or impact ejecta; a script should attribute this as an ongoing scientific debate that MESSENGER helped clarify with volcanic vent evidence.
- Crustal thickness measurements: Mariner 10 and MESSENGER data indicate a thickness of about 35 km, while Airy isostasy models estimate 26 ± 11 km; a script should leave this detail out or attribute it as differing model estimates.
- Cause of the 'Weird Terrain': Competing hypotheses attribute the disrupted terrain opposite Caloris Basin to focused seismic shockwaves or converging ejecta; attribute as 'theorized by scientists'.
