# ep066 research: The Extreme 18-Hour World of 55 Cancri e (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-10-06 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Imagine a world where a whole year lasts less than 18 hours. Located just 40 light-years away, 55 Cancri e is a giant rocky "super-Earth" nearly eight times more massive than our own planet, orbiting so close to its sun-like star that it is trapped in a death spiral. Because it is tidally locked, one side permanently faces the stellar furnace, roasting at temperatures over 2,000 Kelvin—hot enough to melt iron. First detected in 2004, this extreme world has puzzled scientists for two decades. NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope made history by detecting its direct infrared light, revealing wild temperature swings likely caused by massive volcanic eruptions and towering plumes of dust. Most recently, the James Webb Space Telescope peered through the glare to detect a thick, volatile atmosphere rich in carbon dioxide, sustained by a bubbling magma ocean. It is a literal hellscape next door.

## Disputed or left out

- The diamond interior theory is only mentioned in [S1]. The script should treat this as a tentative hypothesis rather than a fact, noting that other studies in [S2] and [S3] suggested it was a water-heavy world, while recent findings in [S4] favor a rocky planet with a magma ocean.
- Early studies [S1, S7] miscalculated the planet's orbital period as roughly 2.8 days, which Dawson and Fabrycky proved in 2010 to be an observational 'alias' of its true 18-hour orbit. The script should explain this as a case of corrected orbital data.
- Early atmospheric analyses yielded highly contradictory results, with some reporting hydrogen/helium/hydrogen cyanide signatures [S1], and others finding no escaping hydrogen or helium. JWST observations in 2024 resolved this by ruling out a rock vapor atmosphere and confirming a volatile atmosphere of carbon oxides.
- The exact distance to the system varies slightly in sources, being cited as 41 light-years in [S2, S5] and 40 light-years in [S3, S6]. The script should refer to it as 'around 40 light-years' away.
