# ep423 research: Why Our Galaxy's Giant Black Hole Is Tiny (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-11-14 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: At the heart of the Milky Way lurks Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole packing four million times the mass of our Sun into a region small enough to fit inside Mercury's orbit. For decades, astronomers inferred its existence by tracking nearby stars whipping around an invisible center at thousands of kilometers per second, work that won a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics. In May 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration revealed the first direct image of Sagittarius A*. By linking eight radio observatories into an Earth-sized virtual telescope, scientists captured light from glowing gas swirling near the speed of light. Because the gas completed orbits in mere minutes, processing five years of data was likened to photographing a puppy chasing its tail. The resulting shadow matched Albert Einstein's general relativity predictions with remarkable accuracy.

## Disputed or left out

- Exact distance to Sagittarius A* varies across sources from 25,000 to 27,000 light-years; scripts should state 'about 26,000 to 27,000 light-years'.
- Exact mass calculations range between 3.6 and 4.3 million solar masses; scripts should round to 'about four million solar masses'.
- The physical nature of the G2 object passing near Sagittarius A* is debated between a pure gas cloud, a star shrouded in gas, or merged binary stars; attribute differing theories if discussed.
