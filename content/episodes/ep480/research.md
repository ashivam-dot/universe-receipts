# ep480 research: Why the Sun Is Hiding in a Million-Degree Bubble (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-11-22 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Fourteen million years ago, a chain reaction of roughly fifteen massive supernova explosions began blasting away interstellar gas, carving out a 1,000-light-year-wide cavity known as the Local Bubble. As the bubble's shockwaves swept outward, they collected cold gas and dust along the shell, triggering the birth of all nearby young stars within 500 light-years of Earth—including regions like the Taurus molecular cloud. Our Sun was not born here. Five million years ago, our solar system randomly drifted right into the void, leaving traces of supernova-born radioactive iron-60 in Earth's deep-sea crust and ice. Today, the Sun sits almost smack in the middle of this superheated, low-density plasma bubble. Though its growth has slowed, data from ESA's Gaia observatory shows the Local Bubble is still coasting outward at four miles per second, continuing to reshape our galactic neighborhood.

## Disputed or left out

- S5 mentions that up to 1,000 supernovas formed the bubble, whereas S1, S4, S6, and S7 attribute it to around 15 (or 14 to 20) supernovas; the script should stick to the consensus figure of about 15 supernovas.
- S1 notes an older hypothesis that a single supernova (Geminga pulsar) created the bubble, but states current research favors multiple supernovae in stellar moving groups; the script should focus on the consensus of multiple supernovae.
