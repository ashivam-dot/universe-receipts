# ep360 research: The Fire That Reshaped NASA (Mission Files)

Researched 2026-11-05 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.1-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In January 1967, astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger Chaffee boarded Apollo 1 for a routine launchpad simulation. Because the rocket was unfueled, NASA considered the test non-hazardous. But inside the command module, the cabin was pressurized with pure oxygen at high pressure. As the countdown halted to troubleshoot communications issues, an electrical arc near vulnerable wiring ignited flammable nylon and Velcro inside the cockpit. Within seconds, extreme pressure ruptured the spacecraft's inner wall and filled the cabin with toxic smoke. The astronauts attempted to open the hatch per emergency procedures, but the inward-opening plug door design was impossible to pull open against the overwhelming internal pressure. All three crew members died of asphyxiation. The tragedy grounded crewed Apollo flights for twenty months. NASA completely overhauled spacecraft safety, redesigning the hatch to open outward in seconds, replacing cabin materials, and introducing a safer nitrogen-oxygen launch atmosphere.

## Disputed or left out

- Exact final words spoken over the radio loop: sources document multiple garbled audio interpretations ('They're fighting a bad fire', 'We've got a bad fire', 'I'm reporting a bad fire'); scripts should avoid quoting specific variations as absolute fact.
- Wally Schirra's pre-flight warning: popular accounts claim Schirra warned Grissom to get out at the first sign of trouble, which contradicts official investigation reports of backup crew debrief satisfaction; scripts should omit this unverified account.
- Post-fire body positions: Deke Slayton's eyewitness account differed from the official board report regarding body placement; scripts should focus on cause of death rather than disputed body positions.
