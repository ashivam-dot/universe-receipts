# ep177 research: The Fastest Probe Ever Launched Carried a Stamp (Mission Files)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1991, the U.S. Postal Service released stamps celebrating planetary exploration. Every world showed a spacecraft, except the ninth, which bore a stinging challenge: 'Pluto: Not Yet Explored.' Rankled scientists used that stamp as a battle cry to make a mission happen. On January 19, 2006, NASA launched New Horizons directly into a solar escape trajectory at an Earth-relative speed of 16.26 kilometers per second, making it the fastest human-made object ever launched from Earth. To ensure the probe hit its mark across three billion miles, astronomers used the ALMA radio telescope array in Chile to precisely calculate Pluto's orbit. After a speed boost from Jupiter and a nine-and-a-half-year journey, New Horizons sped past Pluto on July 14, 2015, traveling at over 52,000 miles per hour. Tucked inside were nine cultural mementos, including that very 1991 'Not Yet Explored' stamp, finally delivered to the edge of the solar system.

## Disputed or left out

- Clyde Tombaugh's ashes: Wikipedia [S1] states that about 30 grams of Pluto discoverer Clyde Tombaugh's ashes are aboard New Horizons, but no other provided source mentions this detail. Under the two-site rule, the script should leave out the ashes claim.
- Record speed distinctions: New Horizons set the record for the fastest launch velocity from Earth, but probes falling toward the Sun (such as Helios and Parker Solar Probe) achieved higher heliocentric or orbital speeds. The script should explicitly clarify it was the fastest object launched from Earth.
- Pluto's planetary classification: While the IAU reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet in 2006, New Horizons team members like Alan Stern dispute the IAU definition and continue to advocate for Pluto as a planet; attribute this debate to mission scientists.
