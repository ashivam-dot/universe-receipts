# ep028 research: The North Star Brightness Myth Busted (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-02 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Many people assume the North Star, Polaris, is the brightest star in the night sky, but it actually ranks around 48th. The brightest star in our sky is Sirius, which appears roughly 25 times brighter than Polaris. Sirius blazes so brightly mainly because it is practically next door—only 8.7 light-years from Earth. By contrast, Polaris sits over 430 light-years away, though it is intrinsically a yellow supergiant 2,500 times more luminous than our Sun. Polaris earned its iconic fame not from brilliant light, but from its unique position aligned directly above Earth's northern rotational axis, making it appear fixed while other stars rotate around it. But even this title is temporary: Earth's 26,000-year axial precession wobble means Polaris will reach its closest northern alignment around 2100, and in 13,000 years, the brilliant star Vega will take over as the North Star.

## Disputed or left out

- Exact numerical distance to Polaris: older modeling and summary sources estimate 430 to 433 light-years, while Hipparcos gives 432 light-years and Gaia DR3 gives 446.5 light-years. Script should state 'over 430 light-years away'.
- Exact visual brightness rank: S1 cites 47th, S2/S4/S5/S7 cite 48th, and S3/S6 cite around 50th. Script should state 'around 48th brightest'.
- Historical change in brightness reported by Ptolemy: S1 notes Ptolemy saw it as third magnitude whereas today it is second magnitude, but notes this is controversial and theoretical models disagree. Script should leave this out.
