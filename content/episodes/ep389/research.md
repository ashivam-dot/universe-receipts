# ep389 research: The Invisible Tug on Sirius (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-11-08 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Sirius is famous for being the brightest star in Earth's night sky, blazing just 8.6 light-years away in the constellation Canis Major. But in 1844, German astronomer Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel noticed a strange wobble in Sirius's path, deducing that an invisible companion was pulling on it. Eighteen years later, on January 31, 1862, American telescope maker Alvan Graham Clark spotted the hidden culprit while testing a giant 18.5-inch lens: a faint companion star now known as Sirius B. In 1915, astronomer Walter Adams analyzed its light and confirmed a mind-bending reality. Sirius B is a white dwarf star—the dead core of an ancient star that was once more massive than Sirius A. It packs roughly the entire mass of our Sun into a sphere smaller than planet Earth, making its material so dense that a single small sample weighs tons on Earth. Today, these two stars orbit each other every 50 years.

## Disputed or left out

- Ancient color of Sirius: Classical scholars like Ptolemy and Seneca described Sirius as red, whereas contemporary Chinese sources recorded it as white. Modern astronomers attribute red reports to atmospheric reddening near the horizon; the script should attribute the red description as an ancient mystery or leave it out.
- Exact weight per unit volume of Sirius B: Estimates in the sources range from 1 ton per cubic inch to 15 tons per cubic inch depending on calculated core vs average density. The script should stick to a general high-impact comparison like 'a single cubic inch weighs over a ton'.
