# ep452 research: When Uranus Was Almost Named King George (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-11-19 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1781, German-born musician William Herschel was scanning the constellation Gemini through a homemade reflecting telescope when he spotted a faint, moving celestial object. Thinking he had found a comet, Herschel tracked its motion until calculations proved it was an undiscovered planet—the first found since antiquity. Herschel, seeking royal favor, named the world Georgium Sidus, or 'George's Star,' in honor of Britain's King George III. The king was flattered, naming Herschel his court astronomer and funding the construction of a colossal 40-foot telescope, while also awarding William's sister and observational partner, Caroline Herschel, the first state salary ever paid to a female astronomer. But astronomers across the globe flatly rejected putting the British king into the heavens, with the French calling it 'Herschel' before the astronomical community settled on the mythological name Uranus. Today, our solar system's seventh planet carries the name of a classical god instead of King George.

## Disputed or left out

- Whether Herschel was knighted immediately after discovering Uranus: S4 states he was knighted following the 1781 discovery, but S1 notes he was made a Knight of the Royal Guelphic Order much later in 1816 and that this was not equivalent to an official British knighthood. A script should avoid claiming an immediate formal knighthood.
- The sole role of the 40-foot telescope in discovering Saturn's moons: S5 notes that attributing the discoveries of Mimas and Enceladus exclusively to the 40-foot instrument is debatable because Herschel operated multiple telescopes simultaneously, whereas S1 links them directly to the 40-foot becoming operational. A script should attribute it simply as discoveries made around that time.
