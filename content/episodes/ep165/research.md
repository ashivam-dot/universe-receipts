# ep165 research: Why Pluto Never Finished Its Orbit (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: When Clyde Tombaugh discovered Pluto at Lowell Observatory in 1930 using a blink comparator, it was celebrated as the Solar System's ninth planet. Named after a suggestion by eleven-year-old Venetia Burney, Pluto soon proved to be a world of contradictions. Smaller than Earth's Moon and seven other solar system moons, Pluto follows an extreme path. Its orbit is tilted over 17 degrees and is so eccentric that it spent 1979 to 1999 closer to the Sun than Neptune. Furthermore, Pluto and its giant moon Charon are mutually tidally locked, endlessly showing each other the same face during their 6.4-day rotation. The discovery of other distant icy bodies, particularly Eris, triggered an astronomical reckoning. In August 2006, the International Astronomical Union formally redefined a planet, requiring a world to clear its orbital neighborhood. Because Pluto shares its realm, it was reclassified as a dwarf planet, a title confirmed when NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past in July 2015.

## Disputed or left out

- Whether Pluto's name inspired Disney's character Pluto: S1 notes Disney introduced the dog in 1930, but animator Ben Sharpsteen could not confirm the exact reason for the name; scripts should treat this as a cultural coincidence or attribute it.
- Comet tail hypothesis: S5 suggests Pluto would grow a tail if moved close to Earth, whereas S1 notes this claim is disputed because Pluto's escape velocity is too high; scripts should leave out the comet tail assertion.
- Atmospheric collapse expectations: S1 and S7 note scientists initially expected Pluto's atmosphere to collapse and freeze onto the surface as it moved farther from the Sun, but flyby and occultation data revealed a persistent and dynamic atmosphere; scripts should frame early collapse predictions as past hypotheses.
