# ep087 research: The Lonely Reality of the Asteroid Belt (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-08 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.5-flash, gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Forget the chaotic asteroid fields in movies. If you flew through the asteroid belt, you wouldn’t be dodging giant rocks. In reality, it is a vast, lonely desert. On average, these space rocks are about 600,000 miles apart—more than twice the distance from Earth to the Moon. In 1801, Giuseppe Piazzi discovered the first fragment, Ceres, while hunting for a 'missing planet' predicted by a mathematical pattern. An informal group called the 'Celestial Police' even formed to track it down. They didn't find a planet, but a graveyard of planetesimals that Jupiter’s gravity prevented from merging. Despite containing millions of objects, the belt’s total mass is less than 4% of our Moon. This extreme emptiness is why NASA probes have cruised through the belt for over 50 years without a single collision. Spacecraft don't need fancy maneuvers; the odds of hitting an asteroid are less than one in a billion.

## Disputed or left out

- The exact mass of the asteroid belt relative to the Moon (S1 and S2 state 3%, while S6 and S7 state 4%; the script should use 'roughly 3 to 4%').
- The percentage of mass contained in the four largest asteroids (S1 states 60-62%, while S6 and S7 state approximately 50%; the script should say 'at least half').
- The exact percentage of original mass lost (S1 states 99.9%, while S7 states 99.99%; attribute as 'over 99%').
- The origin of the word asteroid (S5 mentions a 2008 claim that Stephen Weston invented it, but concludes that William Herschel remains the confirmed creator).
