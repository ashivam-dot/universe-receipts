# ep168 research: The 50-Year Hunt for a Phantom Planet (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1859, French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier faced a puzzle: Mercury was wobbling off its Newtonian orbit by 43 arcseconds per century. Having previously discovered Neptune using mathematical predictions of orbital quirks, Le Verrier deduced another unseen world must be tugging Mercury from inside its orbit. Months later, amateur astronomer and country physician Edmond Lescarbault reported watching a small black silhouette transit across the Sun. Le Verrier rushed to inspect the doctor's homemade observatory, became convinced, and announced the discovery of planet Vulcan, earning Lescarbault the Legion of Honor. For decades, eclipse expeditions and stargazers hunted Vulcan, with several prominent astronomers claiming sightings. Yet the phantom world never existed. In November 1915, Albert Einstein plugged Mercury's orbit into his new theory of general relativity, proving the Sun's immense mass warps spacetime itself. The calculated curvature matched Mercury's precession precisely, instantly dissolving the phantom planet.

## Disputed or left out

- Astronomer Emmanuel Liais claimed he was observing the Sun from Rio de Janeiro with a more powerful telescope at the exact moment Lescarbault reported the transit and saw nothing; handle by attributing: 'According to a rival astronomer observing simultaneously, no object was present.'
- Sources attribute the origin of the name 'Vulcan' differently (Jacques Babinet in 1846 versus Le Verrier in 1859); handle by stating: 'Proposed years earlier, the name Vulcan was formally adopted by Le Verrier.'
- Observers at the 1878 solar eclipse claimed to have seen Vulcan, but rival astronomer C. H. F. Peters disputed the sightings as misidentified stars caused by crude recording devices; handle by attributing: 'Skeptics argued observers had simply mischarted known stars.'
