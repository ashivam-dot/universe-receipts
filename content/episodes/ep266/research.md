Story: For thousands of years, humans gazed at the glowing river arching across the night sky and wondered what it was. Greek mythology claimed it was milk spilled from the goddess Hera's breast, while scholars like Aristotle thought it was burning vapors in Earth's atmosphere. To the unaided human eye, it appeared as nothing more than a seamless, wispy cloud. Everything changed in 1610 when Galileo Galilei aimed his primitive spyglass at the celestial band. To his astonishment, the milky fog vanished, resolving into an overwhelming swarm of countless individual stars packed too tightly and faintly for the naked eye to separate. Today we know our solar system sits inside a massive barred spiral disk containing hundreds of billions of stars and planets, all orbiting a central supermassive black hole with a mass of about 4 million suns. That ghostly splash of milk wasn't a cloud—it was home.

## Disputed or left out

- Herschel's star-counting partner: S2 explicitly attributes the 1785 star counts to sibling astronomers William and Caroline Herschel, while S1 mentions only William Herschel. A script should attribute William or say 'William and Caroline Herschel'.
- Diameter of the Milky Way stellar disk: S1 cites an isophotal diameter of roughly 87,400 light-years, while S2 references modern measurements placing the disk at 120,000 light-years across. A script should state disk estimates vary between roughly 87,000 and 120,000 light-years.
- The Heracles breastfeeding story: S1 notes two variants—one where Zeus secretly places the sleeping infant on Hera's breast, and another where Athena gives him to Hera. A script should simply state: 'According to Greek myth, Hera spilled milk while pushing away baby Heracles.'
