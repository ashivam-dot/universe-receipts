# ep397 research: Why the Universe’s Most Common Stars are Invisible (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-11-11 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Myth: The Sun is a typical star. Busted: Our Sun is actually a rare monster. Up to 80% of stars in the Milky Way are red dwarfs—tiny, dim embers that make the Sun look like a giant. Even our closest neighbor, Proxima Centauri, is one, yet you can’t see it. In fact, not a single red dwarf is bright enough to be seen with the naked eye from Earth. These are the universe’s ultimate marathon runners; while the Sun dies in billions of years, red dwarfs can live for trillions. Not a single one has ever died in the history of the universe. They recycle their fuel through convection, mixing helium through their entire bodies to keep the fire going. They often host Earth-sized worlds, but there’s a twist: these planets are likely tidally locked, trapped in eternal day or frozen night.

## Disputed or left out

- The total percentage of stars that are red dwarfs is cited as 75% according to one account and 80% according to another.
- The exact mass limit for full convection varies between 0.35 and 0.4 solar masses depending on the stellar model.
- Estimates for red dwarf lifespans vary widely, from 80 billion years in one report to trillions of years in others.
- Proxima Centauri's radius is described as 9% of the Sun's radius in one source and one-seventh (approx 14%) of its diameter in another.
- The final evolution state of low-mass red dwarfs is variously described as becoming a white dwarf or eventually a black dwarf.
- The habitability of tidally locked worlds is debated; while some suggest atmospheric freeze-out is a risk, others cite models where heat circulation maintains liquid water.
