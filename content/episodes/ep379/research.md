# ep379 research: The Coldest Planet Isn't the Farthest Away (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-11-08 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1986, NASA's Voyager 2 swept past Uranus, giving humanity its first close-up view of the seventh planet from the Sun. Discovered in 1781 by astronomer William Herschel—who initially mistook it for a comet—Uranus turned out to be one of the strangest worlds in the solar system. Even though Neptune is farther from the Sun, Uranus holds the record for the coldest planetary atmosphere in the solar system, plunging down to a freezing minus 371 degrees Fahrenheit. Wrapped in methane gas that absorbs red light and gives the planet its signature blue-green color, Uranus spins almost completely on its side—likely the result of an ancient collision with an Earth-sized object. Taking 84 Earth years to orbit the Sun, Uranus's extreme tilt subjects its poles to decades of continuous sunlight and darkness. It also rotates backwards, features a lopsided magnetic field, and harbors moons named after Shakespearean characters.

## Disputed or left out

- Duration of seasonal darkness: S1 states each pole experiences 42 years of continuous darkness, while S3 describes a 21-year-long dark winter for half the planet. A script should state generally that the planet experiences decades of polar darkness without locking into conflicting exact figures.
- Early discovery of Uranus's rings: S7 notes William Herschel claimed to observe a ring around Uranus in 1789, though mainstream astronomers dismissed it until the 1977 occultation discovery. A script should treat Herschel's ring observation as a disputed historical account ('according to one account').
