# ep146 research: Halley's Comet Is Burning Above You TONIGHT (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-14 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.7-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Every October, skywatchers gaze up at the Orionid meteor shower, watching bright shooting stars streak across the sky near the constellation Orion. But these streaks are not falling stars at all. They are tiny crumbs shed by humanity's most celebrated cosmic traveler: Halley's Comet. As Halley swings around the Sun on its roughly seventy-six-year retrograde orbit, solar heat vaporizes its dirty ice, scattering streams of grit and pebble-sized dust along its path. Twice each year, Earth plows directly through this cosmic debris trail, igniting the May Eta Aquariids and the October Orionids. Entering the atmosphere at a blistering sixty-six kilometers per second, these ancient particles burn up into brilliant fireballs and persistent glowing trains. Even though Halley's dark nucleus has not visited the inner Solar System since 1986 and will not return until 2061, bits of its frozen crust burn above our heads every single autumn.

## Disputed or left out

- Origin of the Eta Aquariids: while most sources treat them as directly spawned by Halley's debris stream, S7 notes that 1986 observations led some astronomers to suggest the shower might merely be perturbed by the comet rather than originating from it. A script should state they are traditionally linked to Halley.
- Pope Callixtus III excommunicating the comet in 1456: S2 identifies this as an 18th-century invention by an anti-church writer rather than an official historical decree. A script should omit this myth.
- Genghis Khan invading Europe due to Halley's 1222 apparition: S2 treats this as legend, so a script should attribute it as folklore or leave it out.
- Nucleus density measurements: S4 cites a fluffy texture with a density of 0.3 g/cm3, whereas S2 and S7 cite roughly 0.55 to 0.6 g/cm3. A script should describe the nucleus generally as a low-density rubble pile.
