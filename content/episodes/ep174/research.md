# ep174 research: The Weapon That Photographed Earth from Space (Mission Files)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In October 1946, more than a decade before Sputnik, American researchers bolted a modified 35-millimeter newsreel camera into the nose of a captured German V-2 rocket at White Sands, New Mexico. Fired straight up, the repurposed ballistic missile climbed 65 miles above the desert, crossing the threshold of space and reaching five times higher than any previous photograph. Snapping a frame every second and a half, the camera captured the curving horizon of Earth framed against the pure blackness of outer space. With no parachute, the rocket plunged back down and slammed into the desert floor at high speed, obliterating the airframe. But the heavy steel cassette shielding the film survived intact. When researchers retrieved the canister and developed the frames that night, humanity saw Earth from beyond the atmosphere for the very first time—delivered not by a spacecraft, but by a salvaged weapon of war.

## Disputed or left out

- Impact speed of the descending rocket: S2 quotes 500 feet per second, S6 cites NASA data stating over 340 miles per hour, while S3 notes reconstructions cite 150 meters per second and disputes exaggerated claims of 500 to 800 meters per second. Scripts should describe the crash as high speed or attribute the exact rate.
- Condition of the camera body: S2, S4, and S6 report the camera was smashed or shattered on impact, whereas S3 states some recovery accounts describe it as usable except for damage like a missing lens. Scripts should emphasize that the steel film cassette protected the film while the vehicle wrecked.
- Payload equipment configuration: S7 mentions eight movie cameras in the instrument package, while other sources focus on the single 35mm DeVry movie camera. Scripts should refer specifically to the 35mm DeVry camera that produced the surviving space photographs.
