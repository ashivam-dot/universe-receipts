# ep198 research: The 5,000-Light-Year Ray From a Black Hole (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-10-20 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1781, Charles Messier spotted a strange smudge in Virgo, cataloging it as nebula M87. Over a century later in 1918, astronomer Heber Curtis looked through the Lick Observatory telescope and noticed something bizarre: a thin, straight ray shooting out of the object's core. That ray was an energetic plasma jet stretching roughly 5,000 light-years into deep space, powered by a central cosmic engine. When the Hubble Space Telescope and later ground arrays zoomed in, they discovered a gargantuan rotating accretion disk feeding a supermassive black hole with billions of times the mass of the Sun. Traveling at relativistic speeds, knots in the jet even appear to move faster than light due to an optical illusion. In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope finally captured the historic first direct picture of this shadow, turning a faint 18th-century smudge into humanity's iconic gateway to black hole physics.

## Disputed or left out

- Distance and jet length: S1 cites ~53.5 million light-years and a jet length of ~4,900–5,000 light-years, while S3 and S4 cite 55 million light-years, with S4 suggesting the jet could reach 7,000 to 8,000 light-years based on revised distances. Scripts should say 'roughly 53 to 55 million light-years away' and 'at least 5,000 light-years long'.
- Central black hole mass measurements: 1990s Hubble spectroscopic data estimated 2.4 billion solar masses (S1, S4), whereas 2019 EHT measurements derived 6.5 billion solar masses (S1, S3). Scripts should attribute the 6.5-billion figure specifically to the Event Horizon Telescope's horizon-scale measurements.
- Central black hole offset: S1 notes a 2010 claim that M87* was physically displaced 7 parsecs from the galactic center, but subsequent 2011 and 2018 studies determined the apparent offset was caused by brightness fluctuations in the jet. Scripts should omit the offset claim or note it was debunked.
