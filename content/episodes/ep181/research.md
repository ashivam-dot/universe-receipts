Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.7-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: On December 27, 2004, the brightest cosmic blast ever recorded struck Earth. It originated halfway across the galaxy in Sagittarius from SGR 1806-20, an ultradense magnetar just twelve miles wide. Squeezed by a magnetic field of a thousand trillion gauss, the star's rigid crust fractured in a colossal starquake. In a tenth of a second, this rupture discharged more energy than our Sun produces in over 100,000 years. In gamma rays, the flash momentarily rivaled the brightness of the full Moon. Even from 50,000 light-years away, the radiation wave expanded and ionized Earth's upper atmosphere, causing the dead of night to briefly register daylight-level ionization. Scientists determined the magnetar rang like a titanic bell, vibrating ninety-five times a second. Had this pinpoint dead star exploded within ten light-years, its gamma-ray torrent would have stripped away Earth's protective ozone layer and unleashed planetary mass extinction.

## Disputed or left out

- Distance to SGR 1806-20: S1 states 42,000 light-years (13 kiloparsecs), while S4, S5, and S7 cite approximately 50,000 light-years. Script should say 'between 40,000 and 50,000 light-years away' or 'roughly 50,000 light-years away'.
- Solar equivalent energy output: S1 and S2 specify that the 0.1-second burst surpassed 150,000 years of solar output, whereas S4 and S5 state 100,000 years. Script should state 'more energy than the Sun emits in 100,000 to 150,000 years'.
- Distance of the nearest known magnetar to Earth: S1 and S4 cite approximately 9,000 light-years, while S5 quotes an estimate that none are closer than 4,000 to 5,000 light-years. Attribute or use S1/S4's specific cataloged distance: 'at least several thousand light-years away'.
