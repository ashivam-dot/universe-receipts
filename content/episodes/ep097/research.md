# ep097 research: The Star Escaping Our Galaxy at 4 Million MPH (The Universe Is Terrifying)

Researched 2026-10-08 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.5-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Deep in the center of the Milky Way lurks Sagittarius A*, a four-million-solar-mass black hole. Around five million years ago, a binary star system wandered too close to this gravitational trap. Through a violent three-body disruption known as the Hills mechanism, the monster captured one star and hurled the other outward like a cosmic bullet. Today, that banished outcast—cataloged as S5-HVS1—is hurtling through space at nearly four million miles per hour, or over 1,700 kilometers per second. That is more than triple the velocity required to break free from the Milky Way's gravitational pull. Tracked by astronomers using sky surveys and the Gaia space telescope, S5-HVS1 is the cleanest confirmed ejection from our galactic center. With its trajectory pointing straight into the void, this lone star is doomed to wander the empty wastes of intergalactic space forever.

## Disputed or left out

- Origin of all hypervelocity stars: Earlier models assumed all hypervelocity stars originated at the central supermassive black hole, but stars like LAMOST-HVS1 and US 708 originated in the disk or from binary supernova detonations. Scripts should specify that while Sagittarius A* is the primary engine, other ejection mechanisms exist.
- Classification of US 708: S4 identifies US 708 as a helium-rich subdwarf, S5 and S7 describe it as a white dwarf, and S6 calls it an O-type star. Scripts should describe it generally as an extreme high-speed star or avoid specifying its stellar type.
- Birthplace of HE 0437-5439: Early studies suggested it came from the Large Magellanic Cloud, but Hubble astrometry later traced its trajectory back to the Milky Way center. Scripts should present the Large Magellanic Cloud origin as an earlier hypothesis resolved by Hubble.
