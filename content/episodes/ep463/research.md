# ep463 research: Why You Wouldn't Feel a Black Hole's Edge (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-11-20 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1916, Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's equations to find a point of no return—the Schwarzschild radius. Einstein himself spent 1939 trying to prove these 'black holes' were impossible, but Robert Oppenheimer showed they were inevitable. We call this boundary the event horizon, where gravity is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light. If you watched a friend fall in, they would appear to slow down, turn a ghostly red, and freeze at the edge forever. But for the traveler, the experience is different. In your own time, you simply cruise past the event horizon in a few seconds. In a supermassive black hole like the one at our galaxy's center, you wouldn't feel a thing as you cross. You are only doomed once you reach the central singularity—a point of infinite density where all matter is eventually crushed.

## Disputed or left out

- The origin of the term 'black hole' is disputed; while often credited to John Wheeler in 1967, it was used in print as early as 1963 and 1964. The script should attribute the popularization to Wheeler but acknowledge earlier use.
- The 'firewall' hypothesis suggests matter is burned at the event horizon, but this contradicts general relativity's prediction that the traveler feels nothing. The script should attribute this as a controversial quantum theory.
- Stephen Hawking proposed using 'apparent horizons' instead of event horizons; the script should present event horizons as the standard relativistic model while noting theoretical challenges.
- The exact mass limit for neutron stars (Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit) is not fully known due to uncertainties in neutron interactions. The script should attribute the limit of 'about three solar masses' to general models.
