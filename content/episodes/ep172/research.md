# ep172 research: Why the Van Allen Belts Didn't Fry Apollo Astronauts (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-10-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 1958, satellite data from Explorer 1 led physicist James Van Allen to discover two massive radiation belts trapped by Earth's magnetic field. Composed of an inner proton belt and an outer electron belt, these regions raised intense concerns for future space travel. Skeptics argued that passing through these radioactive zones would fry human travelers. However, during the Apollo Moon missions, NASA trajectory planners designed high-speed routes through the thinnest parts of the belts. Protected by the spacecraft's aluminum skin and moving at roughly 25,000 miles per hour, Apollo astronauts spent only about 30 minutes in the dense regions. Dosimeter readings confirmed that their total belt radiation exposure was surprisingly low, comparable to a standard medical CT scan. Though Apollo crews narrowly missed a severe solar storm in August 1972, their fast passage through the Van Allen belts proved that human deep-space travel was possible.
