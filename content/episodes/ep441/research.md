# ep441 research: Why Uranus Smells Like Rotten Eggs (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-11-18 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: For decades after Voyager 2 swept past Uranus in 1986, astronomers were baffled by the hidden chemistry of the planet's pale cyan clouds. In April 2018, an international research team finally solved the mystery using the 8-meter Gemini North telescope on Mauna Kea. Analyzing reflected infrared sunlight, they confirmed that Uranus's upper cloud tops are permeated with hydrogen sulfide—the noxious molecule that gives rotten eggs their signature stench. Unlike Jupiter and Saturn, where ammonia ice dominates the cloud tops, Uranus formed in the colder outer reaches of the early solar system, locking away frozen sulfur. But if you picture an overpowering cosmic stench, human senses would never register it. Uranus's atmosphere is an inhospitable cocktail of hydrogen, helium, and methane plunged to minus 200 degrees Celsius; any unprotected visitor would freeze solid and suffocate long before catching a single whiff.

## Disputed or left out

- The exact altitude and pressure level of Uranus's upper cloud decks remain model-dependent; older radio occultation placed methane cloudtops at 1.2–1.3 bar and lower clouds at 3 bar, whereas newer near-infrared analyses place them between 1–3 bar and up to 6 bar. Scripts should refer broadly to 'the cloud tops' rather than citing a single disputed bar depth.
