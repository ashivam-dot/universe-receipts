# ep436 research: How a Tennis Court in Space Saves Webb (Mission Files)

Researched 2026-11-17 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: The James Webb Space Telescope operates in deep space to detect faint infrared heat signals from the early universe. To see these faint targets, the telescope must stay colder than minus 370 degrees Fahrenheit. To achieve this without active coolants, NASA and its partners built a massive five-layer sunshield roughly the size of a tennis court. Made of ultra-thin Kapton coated with aluminum and silicon, its layers are thinner than a human hair—the front layer is just 0.05 millimeters thick, while its protective metal coatings are measured in nanometers. Folded inside an Ariane 5 rocket for launch on Christmas Day 2021, the sunshield required a nerve-wracking sequence of 139 release mechanisms, 90 cables, and 400 pulleys to unfurl in space. Completed on January 4, 2022, the successful deployment retired over 70 percent of the mission's 344 single-point failures, creating a thermal barrier between a scorching hot side and a deep-freeze cold side.

## Disputed or left out

- Sunshield width dimensions vary slightly across sources: S1, S2, S3, and S4 list dimensions around 21 m by 14 m (approx. 69.5 ft by 46.5 ft), whereas S6 lists width as 54 feet (16 m). Script should state 'roughly 21 by 14 meters' or 'tennis-court sized'.
- Exact percentage of single-point failures retired: S5 quotes a tweet saying 'about 75%', whereas S6 attributes '70 to 75 percent' to project manager Bill Ochs. Script should state 'over 70 percent'.
