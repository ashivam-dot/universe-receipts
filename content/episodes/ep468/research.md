# ep468 research: The Triple Secret of the North Star (Space Myths, Busted)

Researched 2026-11-20 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: Many people believe the North Star, Polaris, is the brightest star in the night sky, but it actually ranks only around 47th or 48th. Its fame comes from standing nearly motionless above Earth's North Pole, anchored by Earth's rotational axis while other stars appear to circle it. But Polaris holds an even bigger secret: it is not a single star. It is a triple star system dominated by Polaris Aa, a yellow supergiant more than two thousand times brighter than our Sun. Orbiting close to it every thirty years is a smaller companion star, Polaris Ab, which was directly photographed for the first time by the Hubble Space Telescope. A third companion, Polaris B, orbits much farther out and was discovered by William Herschel in 1779. Due to Earth's 26,000-year axial precession, Polaris will not remain our North Star forever; by 4000 AD, Gamma Cephei will take its place.

## Disputed or left out

- Exact distance to Polaris (older models state ~430 light-years, Hipparcos states ~432 light-years, Gaia DR3 states ~446.5 light-years, while disputed short-distance models suggested ~323 light-years): attribute by citing modern space telescopes like Gaia or giving a general range of 430–446 light-years.
- Exact mass of Polaris Aa (estimates range from 4.3 to 5.4 solar masses depending on measurement method and orbit models): frame as roughly 4.5 to 5.4 solar masses.
- Pulsation mode of Polaris Aa (whether it is a fundamental or first-overtone pulsator): attribute as a subject of ongoing stellar evolution research if mentioned.
