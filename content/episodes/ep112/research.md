# ep112 research: The Asteroid That Hid in Plain Sight (Near Misses)

Researched 2026-10-09 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash, gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.7-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In July 2019, a massive space rock named 2019 OK caught planetary defenders completely off guard. Roughly the size of a football field, this "city-killer" asteroid was officially announced just hours before its closest approach. It screamed past Earth at nearly 55,000 miles per hour, missing us by less than one-fifth of the distance to the Moon. If it had struck, it could have exploded with over 30 times the energy of the Hiroshima atomic bomb, devastating an area 50 miles wide. Why didn't we see it? Existing sky surveys had actually photographed it weeks prior, but missed it because its highly elliptical orbit had it heading directly towards us. This made it appear completely stationary in the night sky. Thankfully, this Apollo-class asteroid won't return this close for another 200 years, giving humanity time to build better celestial eyes.

## Disputed or left out

- The exact closest approach distance is disputed. Sources S1, S6, and S7 list it around 71,354 kilometers (44,337 miles), S2 and S3 state about 65,000 kilometers (40,000 miles), while S4 and S5 state about 45,000 miles (72,500 kilometers). The script should describe the distance generally as 'between 40,000 and 45,000 miles.'
- Some news sources (S4, S5) attribute the late detection to the asteroid approaching from the direction of the Sun. However, astronomical records (S1, S3, S7) state the actual reasons were its slow apparent motion, Southern Hemisphere positioning, and bright moonlight. The script should focus on its slow apparent motion making it look like a star.
