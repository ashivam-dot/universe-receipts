# ep411 research: Why NASA Turned Voyager 1 Back Toward the Sun (Scale Breaker)

Researched 2026-11-12 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.5-flash-lite, gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.8-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: On Valentine's Day 1990, NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft received a historic command: turn around and look back toward home. Cruising nearly four billion miles away and tilted 32 degrees above the planetary plane, Voyager pointed its optics inward. Astronomer Carl Sagan had campaigned for a decade for this final photo shoot before the probe's imaging system was powered down forever. Over 60 individual exposures, Voyager captured the first outside portrait of our planetary family. The mosaic framed six worlds—Jupiter, Earth, Venus, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune—orbiting our Sun. Mercury and Mars were lost in the Sun's blinding glare, while Pluto was far too faint to detect. To prevent frying its delicate camera sensors, Voyager imaged the blazing central star through its darkest filter with an exposure of five thousandths of a second. From the outer solar frontier, Earth appeared as a fragile crescent smaller than a single pixel.

## Disputed or left out

- Date of acquisition: Sources S1 and S2 list February 14, 1990, while S3 introduces 'February 15, 1990' near its conclusion after initially noting Valentine's Day. Script should state February 14, 1990.
- Distance measurement: S1 and S3 state approximately 6 billion kilometers (3.7 billion miles), whereas S2 states approximately 4 billion miles. Script should state 'roughly four billion miles (six billion kilometers)'.
- Reason Mars was missed: S1 attributes it to spacecraft viewing geometry creating a thin crescent and filter choice, whereas S3 simply groups Mars with Mercury as lost in solar glare. Script should state Mars was missed due to camera exposure limitations and solar glare.
