# ep052 research: The Cosmic Mystery of Tabby's Star (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-10-05 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.5-flash, gemini-3.5-flash-lite) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In 2015, citizen scientists analyzing NASA Kepler data made an astonishing discovery: a star in the Cygnus constellation, KIC 8462852, was behaving erratically. Dubbed Tabby's Star, its brightness dipped by an unprecedented 22 percent. Since a Jupiter-sized planet only blocks 1 percent of a star's light, this massive, irregular flickering ruled out standard planets. Some astronomers speculated we were witnessing an alien megastructure—a massive Dyson swarm harvesting the star's energy. SETI researchers searched for radio signals but heard nothing. To solve the mystery, astronomer Tabetha Boyajian raised over $100,000 on Kickstarter for telescope time. The breakthrough came when observations revealed the star's light dimmed differently across various wavelengths. Because an opaque alien structure would block all light equally, scientists concluded the culprit is natural. Tabby's Star is actually obscured by vast clouds of space dust, potentially shed by disintegrating comets or a shredded exomoon.

## Disputed or left out

- The century-long dimming trend is highly disputed: Bradley Schaefer argued the star faded by 20 percent over a century based on historic plates, but Michael Hippke and Keivan Stassun argued this was an instrumentation artifact. The script should attribute this dispute, stating that while one study suggested a century of fading, others concluded it was an artifact of changing telescope technology.
- The exact distance to the red dwarf companion varies slightly between sources: Wikipedia states 880 AU, while Universe Today states within about 1,000 AU. The script should generalize this distance as around 880 to 1,000 AU.
- The exact maximum dimming recorded by Kepler is reported as up to 20 percent by NASA JPL and Astronomy.com, but up to 22 percent by Wikipedia, Space.com, and Universe Today. The script should state 'up to 22 percent' but acknowledge the slight variation.
