# ep208 research: The Tumbling Mountain That Buzzed Earth (Near Misses)

Researched 2026-10-21 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: In September 2004, a massive asteroid named 4179 Toutatis swept past Earth just four lunar distances away—about 1.5 million kilometers. Despite internet chain emails claiming a sixty-three percent chance of an apocalyptic impact, astronomers were thrilled rather than terrified. Discovered in 1989 and named after the Celtic protector god of Gaul, Toutatis spans over four kilometers and resembles a battered ginger root formed by two coalesced lobes. Stranger still is its motion: unlike planets that rotate around a single fixed axis, Toutatis tumbles chaotically through space like a botched football pass, governed by two independent cycles of roughly 5.4 and 7.4 days. To an observer on its surface, the Sun would rise and set in completely random directions. Ground radar and later China's Chang'e 2 probe captured stunning views of its craters and boulders, confirming our planet is safe from it for centuries.

## Disputed or left out

- Chang'e 2 flyby distance: S1 states a closest distance of 3.2 kilometers, whereas scientific paper S5 states 770 +/- 120 meters from the asteroid's surface. A script should describe it as passing within a few kilometers, noting surface proximity reached under one kilometer.
- Asteroid dimensions: S1 notes approximately 2.5 km in diameter in the lead and S7 lists 2.8 km, while detailed shape modeling in S1, S4, and S5 establishes maximum dimensions of roughly 4.6 to 4.75 km by 1.95 to 2.4 km. A script should describe its elongated, two-lobed dimensions rather than an average spherical diameter.
- 2004 collision threat: Viral internet emails claimed a 63 percent chance of impact, which scientists confirmed was completely baseless, with orbital models showing zero Earth impact probability for over 600 years.
