# ep009 research: The Solar Storm That Powered the Telegraph (Near Misses)

Researched 2026-09-30 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: On September 1, 1859, astronomer Richard Carrington witnessed two blinding white flashes on the Sun. Less than eighteen hours later, a massive coronal mass ejection slammed into Earth's magnetic field, triggering the largest geomagnetic storm in recorded history. Typically, solar eruptions take several days to reach Earth, but an earlier blast had cleared the path. The resulting auroras were so bright that people in the northeastern U.S. could read newspapers at midnight, and Rocky Mountain gold miners woke up to cook breakfast thinking dawn had arrived. The skies glowed blood-red as far south as Cuba and Hawaii. Across North America and Europe, induced electric currents caused telegraph lines to spark, shock operators, and catch fire. In an astonishing twist, telegraph operators in Boston and Portland unplugged their batteries completely and transmitted messages for two hours powered solely by the magnetic storm itself.

## Disputed or left out

- Nitrate spikes in ice cores: Early claims attributed nitrate spikes in Greenland ice cores to the Carrington Event, but subsequent research indicates these spikes correlate with terrestrial forest fires rather than solar proton events (leave out or attribute).
- Geomagnetic storm Dst values: Estimates of the peak Dst index range from -800 nT to -1750 nT (-0.80 to -1.75 μT) due to off-scale readings on 19th-century magnetometers (attribute range).
- Bombay magnetometer interpretation: Researchers debate whether the Bombay magnetometer recorded ring current intensity or nearby auroral electrojet magnetic field perturbations (leave out).
