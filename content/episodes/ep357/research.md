# ep357 research: The Frozen Heart of Pluto (Space Got Weird)

Researched 2026-11-05 by the cloud studio (gemini-3-flash-preview, gemini-3.6-flash) from the sources below, read in full by the studio. Only claims backed by two or more independent sites were allowed into the script.

Story: When NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto in July 2015, astronomers expected to see a cold, geologically dead world covered in craters. Instead, they discovered one of the most active bodies in the outer Solar System. Dominating Pluto's surface is Tombaugh Regio, a giant heart-shaped feature. Its western half, Sputnik Planitia, is a massive basin filled with smooth nitrogen ice that completely lacks impact craters, proving its surface is astonishingly young. Towering alongside these frozen plains are massive mountains made of solid water ice that rise up to 3,500 meters high. Pluto and its largest moon, Charon, perform a unique gravitational dance, remaining tidally locked to each other every 6.4 Earth days. Beneath its icy crust, Pluto may even host a liquid subsurface ocean. Meanwhile, as the dwarf planet moves farther from the Sun, stellar observations show its thin nitrogen atmosphere has experienced a sudden 20 percent drop in pressure.

## Disputed or left out

- The precise rate of Pluto's atmospheric pressure loss is disputed between atmospheric models predicting under 1% drop and stellar occultation observations detecting a 20% drop; attribute the 20% drop directly to occultation measurement studies.
- The hypothesis that Pluto would develop a comet-like tail if placed near the Sun is disputed due to Pluto's high escape velocity; leave out of the script.
- The exact mechanism generating Charon's dark red northern polar region (Mordor Macula) is debated between captured Plutonian atmospheric gas and internal venting; attribute as hypotheses or leave out.
